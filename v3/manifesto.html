The Fabrum manifesto

AI is abundant. Applied intelligence isn’t.

The university isn’t an eternal form. It’s an invention, and it has been reinvented every time the world changed. Here are the turns that got us here, and why we think the next school starts with doing.

Start with 1088 Skip to the vision

Nine centuries, eight turns

“The question is not whether universities should modernise their syllabuses. It is whether the university, as a social operating system, still matches the way value is created.”

Each version of the university answered a question its own time was asking. Medieval cities needed people who could make sense of law. Industrial nations needed research and experts. Post-war economies needed graduates by the million. Every answer worked, and then the world moved on.

1088Bologna

Bologna: a student reads the law aloud; another carries the purse that pays the teacher.

1088Bologna

The students hired the teachers.

The University of Bologna dates itself to around 1088. No king or bishop founded it. Students formed their own associations, decided what they wanted taught and paid their teachers directly.1 What they wanted was law: Europe’s new cities, the Church and the Empire were pulling against each other, and Bologna’s jurists were asked to settle it.1

What it left usLearners organised around a live problem and paid for teaching that helped them solve it.

c. 1200Paris and Oxford

Masters, charters and a royal ban.

Paris grew out of the cathedral school of Notre-Dame as a guild of masters rather than students. In 1200, after a deadly fight between students and townspeople, King Philip Augustus issued a charter putting students under church courts instead of his own officers.23 Oxford had teaching by 1096 and grew fast after 1167, when Henry II banned English students from going to Paris.4

What it left usThe form travelled. Lectures, the licence to teach and the self-governing guild became the template for centuries.

1810Berlin

Teaching meets research.

Wilhelm von Humboldt, then the Prussian minister responsible for education, founded the University of Berlin in 1809–10. It became known for its modern curriculum and its specialised research institutes, where many basic techniques of laboratory work were pioneered. By 1840 it had more than 1,750 students and was the largest university in Germany.5

What it left usKnowledge became something you make as well as something you inherit. The research university powered the industrial age.

1862United States

Colleges for the farm and the workshop.

On 2 July 1862 the Morrill Act gave US states federal land to fund colleges teaching agriculture and the mechanic arts, “to promote the liberal and practical education of the industrial classes”. It opened higher education to farmers and working people who had been shut out, and Cornell, Nebraska and Clemson were among the schools chartered under it. Much of that land had been taken from Native nations.6

What it left usPractical work earned its place in the university.

1908Boston

Business becomes a subject.

Harvard’s Graduate School of Business Administration opened in 1908 with 15 faculty and 33 regular students: the world’s first MBA programme. From 1924 the case method was its main way of teaching, so students argued through decisions that real managers had already made.7

What it left usBusiness could be studied. Mostly by reading about other people’s decisions, though, not by making your own.

1944After the war

A degree for millions.

The GI Bill, signed on 22 June 1944, paid tuition and living costs for returning US veterans. About 2.3 million of them went to college or university under it, and the number of degrees awarded in the US more than doubled between 1940 and 1950.8

What it left usHigher education became mass education, and the degree became the usual way into a professional job.

2011–12Online

Free to everyone. Finished by few.

In autumn 2011 Stanford put an introduction to artificial intelligence online for free and more than 160,000 people signed up. Udacity and Coursera were founded within months, and MIT and Harvard formed edX.9 Finishing was the catch: of more than 94,000 people who registered for one early Udacity course, just over 10% earned a certificate.10

What it left usContent stopped being scarce. Doing the work, and finishing it, didn’t.

2026London

One person, with AI, and a customer.

The cost of running an AI model as good as GPT-3.5 fell more than 280-fold between November 2022 and October 2024.11 92% of UK undergraduates now use AI, yet only 36% say their university has helped them build AI skills.12 With AI, one person can build and sell what used to need a big team or a big round. What’s scarce is the judgement to point it at a real problem, and the nerve to ask a stranger to pay.

Where that leaves usThis is where Fabrum starts.

What’s scarce now.

“Information is cheap. First drafts are cheap. Many technical tasks are becoming cheap. What remains scarce is judgement: choosing important problems, understanding people, making trade-offs, taking responsibility, combining disciplines, earning trust and turning ambiguous possibilities into useful reality.”

AI does not make human capability irrelevant. It makes unpractised human capability brutally visible.

A transcript records what someone studied. It rarely shows whether they can find a real problem, speak to users, build a solution, use AI responsibly, persuade someone to care or deliver an outcome.

Made, not taught.

We designed Fabrum to close the gap between potential and proof. The unit of learning is not the lecture. It is the loop:

  1. 01See a problem
  2. 02Form a hypothesis
  3. 03Act
  4. 04Meet reality
  5. 05Learn
  6. 06Improve
  7. 07Produce evidence

At Fabrum the loop is a mission: a real-world task matched to your stage, finished with a short proof video and, for anything you sell, a stranger’s payment in the sales log. There are no exams.

It isn’t only a hunch. Across 225 studies, students in lecture-based courses failed at 33.8%, against 21.8% in courses built on active learning.13

What a Fabrum education should produce

  • See important problems before they become obvious.
  • Understand users, not just describe markets.
  • Use AI across research, building, communication and decisions, with judgement.
  • Turn a vague opportunity into a tested product or venture.
  • Persuade, sell, recruit and earn trust.
  • Show credible evidence of what they built and what changed because of it.

The vision

We’re building the most applied university in the world.

“By 2125, Fabrum is the world’s most meritocratic and applied institution for developing high-agency entrepreneurial leaders: the people who create the companies, lead the organisations and solve the hardest real-world problems of the AI age.”

That’s our hundred-year goal. Harvard, Stanford, Oxford and Y Combinator are reference points, not the definition of success. The path starts small: a 12-month programme in London, then an accredited MSc, more master’s degrees and bachelor’s degrees, and in time a business school with its own degree-awarding powers.

What we commit to

  • Missions, not lectures.You do real work from the first week, matched to your stage.
  • Proof, not exams.You’re graded on what you did: a short proof video, and a stranger’s payment for anything you sell.
  • No equity.Whatever you build is yours.
  • A cohort you meet in person.A small group in London, chosen for drive, alongside work or study.
  • Only verified results.No income claims, no screenshots of someone’s best month. If we can’t verify it, we won’t show it.

We started where the loop starts: in November 2026 we ran the 48h Startup Challenge in London, in collaboration with UCL School of Management. Forty-eight hours of finding buyers, building with AI and asking for the sale.14

Cohort 1 starts in London in October 2027.
